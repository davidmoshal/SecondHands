SecondHands Project Brief

One on-device movement and body-assessment platform. A camera, a Snapdragon processor, and ExecuTorch turn standardized clinical tests into measured, repeatable results without sending a single frame to the cloud. The clinicians on the team define the tests and the pass marks; the engineers make the measurement fast, cheap, and private.

Status, 17 October 2026: the pose pipeline runs through ExecuTorch on the CPU and is being converted for the Snapdragon NPU with Qualcomm's help. Every test protocol, threshold, and benchmark below is a placeholder until the clinicians set it. This page is a shared picture, not a specification; the repository Wiki holds the specification.

1 · What it is

A test engine, not a fitness app

The platform does one thing well: it watches a person perform a defined movement and turns the body's keypoints into numbers a clinician trusts. Everything else is a protocol on top of that engine. A squat count, a 30-second chair stand, a one-leg balance hold, and a walk-and-turn line test are all the same machinery with different rules and different pass marks.

2 · Use cases

The same engine, five settings

The hackathon demonstrates the first family. The others are the reason the platform is worth building, and each carries its own validation and ethics requirements, noted on the card.

Clinical movement assessment demo

Standardized functional tests scored from the camera instead of a stopwatch and a clipboard: chair stands in 30 seconds, single-leg stance time and sway, timed up and go, joint range of motion. Baseline on day one, retest at follow-up, and a trend the clinician can read in ten seconds.

Protocols and thresholds: the clinicians. Validation: against stopwatch, goniometer, and clinician rating on the same session.

Rehabilitation and fitness progress demo

The Mayo five-step program made measurable: assess, plan, start, track. Guided exercise sets with rep counts and form cues, adapted for people with limited mobility, with progress against their own baseline rather than a population norm.

Builds directly on the assessment protocols; the adapted exercises come from the clinicians.

Field sobriety support later

The standardized walk-and-turn and one-leg-stand tests scored from a body camera or a phone held by a second officer: step count, heel-to-toe gaps, arm raise, sway, hop, and timing, logged with the video. Horizontal gaze testing would need the face model rather than body pose.

Decision support for the officer and a consistent record, not evidence on its own. Needs validation studies, consent and retention rules, and bias testing across body types, footwear, and lighting before any field use.

Skin and body-surface screening later

A private, on-device skin check: photograph a region, map moles and marks, flag asymmetry, size change, or new bumps against the previous visit, and hand the comparison to a clinician. A different model from pose (lesion detection and segmentation) on the same ExecuTorch runtime and the same privacy rules.

Screening and change tracking, never diagnosis. Images stay on the device and are encrypted at rest. The hackathon demo uses a public dermatology dataset, never participants' own images.

Assistive control original SecondHands

The gesture and facial-expression control layer the project started with: a person maps the movements they can perform to switches, lamps, and appliances. It shares the camera, the models, and the dashboard, and remains an extension of the platform.

Existing code and trigger engine are reused as is.

Other settings worth a protocol ideas

Fall-risk screening at home for older adults, desk ergonomics, sports technique coaching, remote physiotherapy check-ins, occupational fitness tests, and clinic triage where a specialist is hours away.

Each is a protocol file plus a validation plan, which is the point of the design.

7 · Reach

Laptop this weekend, phones next, low cost by design

Why it travels to phones

ExecuTorch is built for mobile. The same PyTorch model exports to a .pte for a Snapdragon phone, with the Qualcomm backend targeting that phone's chipset instead of the laptop's. The protocol files, the scoring code, and the report format do not change. What changes is a re-export per chipset and an Android or iOS shell around the runtime.

A phone camera is enough for every protocol on this page. Depth sensors are optional precision, not a requirement.

Why it is cheap

  • No cloud, no per-use fee, no connectivity requirement. It works in a clinic with no internet.
  • Open-weight models and open-source runtime. The only licence to watch is the AGPL on the Ultralytics fallback.
  • One mid-range Snapdragon phone replaces a stopwatch, a goniometer, a clipboard, and the travel to a specialist.
  • Quantized models on the NPU use a fraction of the power of a CPU run, so sessions do not drain the battery.

What that enables

Community health workers running standardized functional tests at home visits; rehabilitation follow-up without a weekly clinic trip; triage in regions where a specialist is a day away; the same measurements in Hyderabad, Nairobi, and San Francisco, read by whichever clinician reviews the report.

8 · Rules we hold ourselves to

Privacy, safety, and honesty

  • Frames stay on the device. Keypoints and scores are stored; video is not, unless a clinician starts an explicit, consented recording.
  • Screening and support, not diagnosis or evidence. Every report says so. Sobriety support is a record for the officer and the court, never an automated verdict.
  • Bias is a test, not a disclaimer. Validation covers body types, skin tones, clothing, mobility aids, lighting, and camera placement before any claim is made.
  • Sensitive imagery is never demonstrated on people. The skin-check flow uses public dermatology datasets at the hackathon.
  • Claims match evidence. Today: the pose model runs through ExecuTorch on the CPU, verified numerically. NPU, phone, and every clinical threshold are work in progress and are labelled that way.
